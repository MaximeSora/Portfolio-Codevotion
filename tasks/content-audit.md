# Portfolio Content Audit
## Head of Design Review

**Reviewer perspective:** Head of Design at a top-tier product company (Stripe/Figma/Linear caliber), evaluating whether to advance this Senior Product Designer to the next interview round.

**Overall first impression:** This portfolio shows a designer who has done real work in complex environments. The process rigor is visible. But the writing consistently undersells the work. Almost every case study reads like a project summary rather than a persuasion piece. The designer describes *what happened* but rarely makes me feel *why it mattered*. At a senior level, I need to see judgment, influence, and the ability to frame decisions in business terms. The raw material is strong, but the packaging is not yet at the bar.

## Case Study 1: MyPowerbox - Mobile App MVP
**Company:** Renault Digital / Mobilize | **Year:** 2023

### Scores (1-5)

| Criterion | Score | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 2 | Opening is descriptive, not compelling. "Designing the first official Mobilize mobile app around a physical home-charging product meant turning hardware constraints..." reads like a project brief, not a hook. |
| Problem framing | 3 | The four-risk framework is solid structure, but the risks read like a checklist rather than a story about real tension. |
| Decision clarity | 3 | Decisions are listed clearly (daily-use navigation, pairing flow, motion work). But the *why* behind each is thin. Why daily-use over setup-centric? What data or insight drove that? |
| Impact proof | 2 | "MVP launched and deployed to app stores" is table stakes. Every app ships. The reuse for V2 is more interesting but buried and not quantified. |
| Strategic thinking | 3 | The white-label angle and design system foundations show broader thinking, but they are mentioned rather than explored. |
| Differentiation | 2 | This reads like many "designed a mobile app" case studies. The IoT angle is the unique hook, but it is not leveraged enough. |

### Top 3 Strengths
1. The guerrilla test findings are specific and credible (6 concrete usability problems, including the charging wheel misinterpretation). This is the kind of evidence that makes me trust the designer.
2. The IoT constraint angle is genuinely interesting. Designing around a physical device creates real design challenges that most portfolios cannot show.
3. The personae work (Early adopter vs. Gradual discovery) shows the designer grounded decisions in behavioral differences, not assumptions.

### Top 3 Weaknesses
1. The headline and summary are interchangeable with any "designed a mobile app" case study. The unique angle (first product for a new brand, hardware dependency, IoT UX) is buried.
2. Impact is the weakest section. "MVP launched" is not proof of design value. What happened after launch? Did engagement change? Did pairing success rates improve? Even directional signals would help.
3. The case study is too long for what it delivers. The toggle sections with archived process details feel like padding. A tighter story with stronger punchlines would land harder.

### Rewrites

**Current headline:**
"Launched the first official Mobilize mobile app around a connected charging product, turning hardware constraints into a usable daily experience."

**Rewritten headline:**
"Mobilize had never shipped a mobile product. I designed the first one around a physical charging station, where half of test users couldn't even pair the device."

**Current summary:**
"Designed the UI of MyPowerbox, one of the first official Mobilize mobile apps, helping translate a physical home-charging product into a usable daily experience and an initial mobile design foundation for the brand."

**Rewritten summary:**
"MyPowerbox was Mobilize's first mobile app, built around a connected home charger. The challenge wasn't just UI: the product depended on Bluetooth pairing with a physical device, an emerging brand identity with no mobile precedent, and a budget that left no room for second chances. I owned the interface end-to-end, from navigation architecture to motion design, and shipped an MVP that became the foundation for V2 and for Mobilize's mobile design system."

**Current challenge:**
"Designing one of the first official Mobilize mobile experiences meant turning a technically constrained charging-station product into a clear day-to-day app, while shaping an emerging brand with limited budget and white-label ambitions."

**Rewritten challenge:**
"The app needed to do three things at once: make a Bluetooth-dependent charging station feel simple to use daily, establish visual credibility for a brand that had never shipped a consumer mobile product, and remain flexible enough for future white-label deployment. When I ran guerrilla tests on the first prototype, half of users couldn't navigate through parameters, and the charging wheel was misread as a button. The pairing flow was the most sensitive area because every failure point was tied to a physical device limitation I couldn't redesign."

**Current impact:**
"Contributed to the launch of the MVP on app stores, with the concept validated through testing and reused as a foundation for V2 and for the early Mobilize mobile design system."

**Rewritten impact:**
"The MVP shipped to iOS and Android and was validated through testing. More importantly, three things outlasted the initial release: the navigation model was carried forward into V2, the component library became the starting point for Mobilize's mobile design system, and the V2 roadmap expanded into a broader MyEnergy platform connecting home charging to Mobilize's wider energy ecosystem. For a first product on a constrained budget, the real proof of design value was that nothing had to be thrown away."

## Case Study 2: FleetUI - Web App Design
**Company:** Renault Digital / Mobilize | **Year:** 2023-2024

### Scores (1-5)

| Criterion | Score | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 2 | "Transforming a fragmented internal fleet tool into a credible B2B SaaS dashboard platform" is accurate but generic. Every B2B redesign pitch starts this way. |
| Problem framing | 4 | The systemic framing ("the problem was not screen by screen, the problem was systemic") is the strongest single line in the portfolio. It shows platform thinking. |
| Decision clarity | 3 | Pattern library vs. screen-by-screen approach is a clear design decision. But the rationale stays abstract. What made pattern-based design the right bet here? |
| Impact proof | 2 | "Improved usability" and "established a scalable dashboard pattern library" are claims without evidence. No metrics, no user quotes, no adoption data. |
| Strategic thinking | 4 | Identifying that the product needed to shift from internal-tool logic to B2B platform logic shows strong product instinct. The three core values (availability, reliability, comfort) show framing ability. |
| Differentiation | 3 | The competitive context (Geotab, Samsara) helps position the work. But the case study doesn't show how the design specifically competed against those benchmarks. |

### Top 3 Strengths
1. The systemic diagnosis is the best framing in the portfolio. "The product was not broken screen by screen. The problem was systemic" immediately signals senior-level thinking.
2. Identifying the opportunity independently ("I identified the need for this product after learning that existing tools...") shows initiative and product sense, not just execution.
3. The competitive positioning against Geotab and Samsara grounds the work in market reality, which most case studies skip.

### Top 3 Weaknesses
1. No measurable impact. This is a significant project (B2B platform redesign) with zero numbers. Even internal metrics (task completion time, support ticket reduction, adoption of dashboard patterns) would help.
2. The "What I changed" section lists five interventions at the same level of detail. Some of these (task-oriented navigation, reusable patterns) are strategic; others (framing product vision) are vague. Hierarchy is missing.
3. The case study doesn't show a single screen or interaction in enough detail to evaluate design quality. The writing carries all the weight, and the writing alone isn't specific enough.

### Rewrites

**Current headline:**
"Helped turn a fragmented fleet tool into a more credible B2B dashboard platform for administrators and managers."

**Rewritten headline:**
"Fleet managers were switching between 4 disconnected tools to do basic monitoring. I restructured the product into a single dashboard platform that could compete with Geotab and Samsara."

**Current summary:**
"Designed the UX and UI foundations of FleetUI, helping transform a fragmented internal tool into a clearer B2B dashboard platform for fleet administrators and managers."

**Rewritten summary:**
"FleetUI started as an internal ops tool with no UX foundation. Fleet managers couldn't complete basic tasks without switching between disconnected systems. I ran a heuristic evaluation and user interviews, then restructured the product around task-oriented navigation and reusable dashboard patterns. The goal was not a redesign but a platform shift: from internal tooling to a B2B SaaS product credible enough to compete in a market where Geotab and Samsara already had mature experiences."

**Current impact:**
"Helped structure a more coherent product vision, improved usability around key fleet-management tasks, and created reusable dashboard patterns to support a more scalable B2B product foundation."

**Rewritten impact:**
"The project moved the product from disconnected internal tooling toward a coherent B2B dashboard foundation. Specifically: navigation was restructured around operational workflows instead of data sources, a pattern library of dashboard components (data cards, status badges, KPI layouts, responsive tables) made future feature design systematic rather than ad hoc, and the team aligned on three core product values (availability, reliability, comfort of management) that gave the roadmap a clearer direction. The strongest outcome was structural: new features could now be built on shared patterns instead of isolated screen designs."

## Case Study 3: Citadel Team - Mobile & Web App (Video Conferencing)
**Company:** Thales Ercom | **Year:** 2021

### Scores (1-5)

| Criterion | Score | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 3 | The opening paragraph names the core tension (action clarity + layout scalability). But it is phrased as a description, not a provocation. |
| Problem framing | 4 | Framing the problem as "action clarity under pressure" is strong. The insight that every fraction of a second spent interpreting controls is a UX failure shows real understanding of the domain. |
| Decision clarity | 4 | The icon redesign rationale is well-articulated: treating icon clarity as a usability issue, not cosmetic. The grid system rules (1:1, 3-4, 5-8, 9+) show systematic thinking. |
| Impact proof | 2 | "The feature set was shipped and continued to evolve" is weak. No clarity on what improved for users. Guerrilla test results are mentioned but not quantified. |
| Strategic thinking | 3 | The product credibility angle (conferencing as differentiator vs. pure messaging competitors) is good but underdeveloped. |
| Differentiation | 3 | The security-product context and solo-designer-on-conferencing angle are differentiating, but they are treated as background facts rather than narrative hooks. |

### Top 3 Strengths
1. Domain-specific UX insight: "In a conferencing context, every fraction of a second spent interpreting controls is a UX failure." This is the kind of line that makes hiring managers nod.
2. The scalable grid system with specific rules for different participant counts shows a designer who thinks in systems, not screens.
3. Solo design ownership on a critical product surface demonstrates autonomy and accountability.

### Top 3 Weaknesses
1. The guerrilla test findings are referenced but never shown with numbers. "Mute recognition on desktop" was tested, but what was the result? What was the before/after recognition rate?
2. The security context (Thales, security-conscious organizations) is a massive differentiator that is barely mentioned. This is not "just another conferencing app." It is used by defense and government organizations. That constraint shapes everything.
3. The outcome is the weakest section. "Shipped and continued to evolve" is not evidence of design quality. What specific user problems were solved? Did the conferencing feature close competitive gaps?

### Rewrites

**Current headline:**
"Improved mobile and web videoconferencing usability by making actions clearer and layouts more scalable as participant counts changed."

**Rewritten headline:**
"Citadel Team's conferencing feature was losing credibility against Zoom and Teams. I redesigned the controls and layout system as the solo designer, so actions were clear under pressure and the grid held up from 2 to 10+ participants."

**Current challenge:**
"The original video conferencing experience was difficult to scale as participant counts changed, while key UI actions and icons were not always intuitive across mobile and web."

**Rewritten challenge:**
"Video conferencing was Citadel's differentiator against pure messaging competitors, but the UX undermined that positioning. Controls had insufficient contrast between active and inactive states. Icon metaphors required interpretation instead of being immediate. The layout degraded beyond 4 participants with no system for grid transitions. In a real-time, security-sensitive context where users cannot afford to pause and think, these were not polish issues. They were trust issues."

**Current impact:**
"Delivered a clearer and more scalable conference experience, improving usability and giving the product team a stronger base for future iterations."

**Rewritten impact:**
"The redesigned controls shipped across mobile and web and became the foundation the team continued building on after my engagement ended. The grid layout system handled participant transitions from 1:1 to 10+ without requiring per-scenario design work. Guerrilla testing validated that mute recognition on desktop and icon clarity on mobile met the bar for real-time usability. The feature moved from being a functional add-on to a credible differentiator for security-conscious organizations evaluating Citadel against mainstream competitors."

## Case Study 4: Citadel Team - Design System Implementation
**Company:** Thales Ercom | **Year:** 2021-2022

### Scores (1-5)

| Criterion | Score | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 3 | "Building Thales Ercom's first design system as a solo designer" is clear and specific. The "solo designer" angle creates interest. |
| Problem framing | 4 | The cost framing is excellent: "every component handoff required explanation, every QA review required re-verification." This translates design-system work into business impact. |
| Decision clarity | 4 | The distinction between "a Figma library alone was not enough" and the need for documentation, tracking, and rituals shows mature thinking about adoption vs. creation. |
| Impact proof | 2 | "Raised design and development maturity" is vague. How? Measured how? Reduced QA loops by how much? Even estimates would be credible. |
| Strategic thinking | 4 | Framing the design system as "an alignment problem, a documentation problem, and a collaboration problem" rather than a UI problem is exactly the kind of perspective I want from a senior designer. |
| Differentiation | 3 | The "building from near-zero maturity" angle differentiates this from "maintained existing design system" stories. But it could be pushed further. |

### Top 3 Strengths
1. The cost-of-inconsistency framing is powerful: "the same input component existed in 4 different visual variations across screens." This makes the problem concrete and business-relevant.
2. Thinking about sustainability from day one ("I had to create something that could survive beyond my involvement") shows responsibility beyond the deliverable.
3. The specific finding that "the same button could look and behave differently across screens, with no way to determine which version was correct" is vivid and memorable.

### Top 3 Weaknesses
1. No quantification of impact. Even rough signals matter: "Reduced QA back-and-forth from X rounds to Y" or "Component handoff time decreased."
2. The case study is well-structured but could differentiate more from a generic "I built a design system" narrative. What was specific to a security product environment? What was unique about building a system while migrating from Sketch to Figma?
3. The "What I would do differently" section (push for Storybook, simpler onboarding, clearer contribution rules) is the most interesting part because it shows real learning. But it is hidden in a toggle and barely developed.

### Rewrites

**Current headline:**
"Built Thales Ercom's first design system to create a shared language between design and development."

**Rewritten headline:**
"The same button looked different on every screen, and nobody knew which version was right. I built Thales Ercom's first design system from zero, alone, to fix a collaboration problem disguised as a UI problem."

**Current impact:**
"Established a documented source of truth that improved team maturity, reduced ambiguity in component handoff, and helped design and development collaborate with a more consistent shared language."

**Rewritten impact:**
"The system went from nonexistent to operational: a full Figma library with variants and states, ZeroHeight documentation with usage rules and implementation notes, page templates, and tracking across design-documented-implemented status. The most telling result was behavioral. QA conversations shifted from 'which version is correct?' to 'here is the reference.' The system continued evolving after my involvement, which was the real test. I also recommended Storybook adoption to close the gap between static documentation and live implementation, a step I would push for earlier if I did this again."

## Case Study 5: Home Charging Quotation Tool
**Company:** Renault Digital / Mobilize Power Solutions | **Year:** 2025

### Scores (1-5)

| Criterion | Score | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 4 | The opening paragraph immediately names the user problem and the business gap. The stat "only 3 out of 18 participants found the relevant page" is a strong hook. |
| Problem framing | 5 | Framing as a two-part problem (discoverability then comprehension) is sharp and specific. This is the best problem framing in the portfolio. |
| Decision clarity | 4 | The two-step product approach (findability then usability) shows strategic sequencing. Individual decisions (guidance, price logic, partner routing) are clear. |
| Impact proof | 5 | Real metrics: 1,504 users, 82 qualified leads, 89% step completion, 90% traffic from targeted entry points. This is the strongest impact section by far. |
| Strategic thinking | 5 | Framing the tool as both a lead engine and a price-discovery experience, even for non-converting users, shows product maturity. The insight about embedding at the point of intent is business-strategic. |
| Differentiation | 4 | The combination of user research, conversion design, and legal/technical constraints in an EV context is genuinely distinctive. |

### Top 3 Strengths
1. This is the only case study with hard metrics. 89% step completion and 82 qualified leads in 6 weeks gives concrete proof of design value.
2. The two-phase problem framing (discoverability then comprehension) is the most sophisticated narrative structure in the portfolio.
3. The insight that even non-converting users got value (anxiety reduction) shows product thinking beyond conversion funnels.

### Top 3 Weaknesses
1. The role description ("My main contribution was not to invent the whole product from scratch, but to make it clearer, more contemporary, more usable, and more deliverable") undersells the work. It sounds like a refinement contribution when the metrics suggest much more.
2. The "What I changed" section lists six interventions without clear hierarchy. The two-step approach is the most important one, but it gets the same weight as "responsive design."
3. The technical concepts (monophase, triphase, bidirectional charging) are named but not explained enough for a portfolio reader who doesn't know EV infrastructure. A brief contextual sentence would help.

### Rewrites

**Current headline:**
"Reduced uncertainty around home charging installation by turning a technical EV buying step into a clearer conversion path."

**Rewritten headline:**
"Only 3 out of 18 users could find the charging offer on Renault's website. I redesigned the quotation experience and it generated 82 qualified leads in its first 6 weeks."

**Current role summary:**
"Focused on UI refinement, affordance, handoff, and stakeholder alignment to keep the journey actionable."

**Rewritten role summary:**
"I owned the design of the quotation flow from research through delivery: user testing, UI redesign, technical guidance patterns, pricing logic, responsive implementation, legal alignment, and partner-routing logic."

**Current challenge:**
"EV buyers faced pricing uncertainty around home charging installation, while Renault and Dacia websites did not make the offer easy enough to find or understand. Mobilize Power Solutions needed a more visible, more comprehensible, and more actionable experience."

**Rewritten challenge:**
"Two problems, in sequence. First, discoverability: the charging offer was invisible inside the existing site architecture. An initial study showed only 3 out of 18 participants could find it unaided. Second, comprehension: once users reached the tool, technical concepts like single-phase vs. three-phase charging and opaque pricing logic created friction. The quotation experience needed to solve both problems, and in that order, because a great tool that nobody finds is a great tool that generates zero leads."

## Case Study 6: Financing Simulator - Reshape 2026
**Company:** Renault Digital / Mobilize Financial Services | **Year:** 2025-2026

### Scores (1-5)

| Criterion | Score | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 3 | The opening sets up the complexity well but does not create urgency. It lists constraints without making me feel the tension. |
| Problem framing | 4 | The four-risk framework applied to a financial product is well done. The friction-first framing ("the redesign started from product friction, not from a visual refresh") is strong. |
| Decision clarity | 4 | Sticky price tracking, compact components, modular product direction: clear, named decisions with rationale. The AI workflow section adds texture without becoming a tangent. |
| Impact proof | 2 | "Work in progress" is honest but leaves the impact section carrying no weight. The case study needs to lead harder with the problems solved, since outcomes are pending. |
| Strategic thinking | 5 | This is the most strategically mature case study. The modular product vision (discovery paths, simulation receipt, lead generation, dealer-locator integration) shows someone thinking at a product-strategy level. Using test evidence as political leverage across countries is a senior move. |
| Differentiation | 4 | The combination of accessibility, multi-country politics, AI-assisted prototyping, and financial-product constraints is distinctive. Few portfolios show this breadth of challenge. |

### Top 3 Strengths
1. The modular product vision is the strongest piece of strategic thinking in the portfolio. Moving the simulator from an isolated calculator to a reusable product component shows product-strategy ability.
2. The AI workflow section is honest and practical: "The goal was not to replace product thinking, but to move faster on execution-heavy tasks." This is the right way to talk about AI in a portfolio.
3. The political navigation (using test evidence to move conversations with countries, legal, and leadership) demonstrates influence and organizational savvy.

### Top 3 Weaknesses
1. No shipped results yet. The case study needs to lean even harder into the decisions and rationale to compensate. Right now it reads slightly like a project update rather than a completed story.
2. The "Status: In validation" label is necessary but should be more prominent and framed as a feature, not a disclaimer. "Here is how I approach work that hasn't shipped yet" is a valid portfolio angle if owned.
3. The case study tries to cover too much ground (discovery, redesign, accessibility, testing, AI, multi-country alignment). Each section is thin because there are too many sections. Prioritizing two or three strongest angles would create a more memorable piece.

### Rewrites

**Current headline:**
"Made Renault and Dacia financing easier to understand and act on while pushing the simulator toward a more modular product model."

**Rewritten headline:**
"Renault's financing simulator gave users a monthly price but not enough clarity or confidence to act on it. I led the redesign to make it more understandable, more accessible, and more useful as a conversion and lead-generation tool across multiple countries."

**Current summary:**
"Redesigning Renault and Dacia's financing simulator to make financing easier to understand, easier to act on, and more conversion-oriented, while balancing accessibility, legal constraints, multi-country complexity, and modular product ambitions."

**Rewritten summary:**
"The financing simulator was the most-used tool on Renault's vehicle pages, but discovery testing showed it wasn't doing enough. Parameters confused users, price evolution was invisible, and the experience stopped at a number instead of guiding toward a next step. I led the redesign across discovery, prototyping, A/B testing, and accessibility audit. The target direction shifts the simulator from a rigid calculator to a modular product that can serve discovery, end-of-configuration, and lead generation, all while meeting legal requirements across countries."

**Current impact:**
"Work in progress. The project has already clarified the main user and business blockers, established a more modular target direction, and is being validated through moderated testing before rollout."

**Rewritten impact:**
"This project is in validation. Final metrics will follow rollout. What the work has already produced: a discovery test that identified the five key user blockers on the current simulator, a strategic direction validated by design leadership, two tested layout variants with moderated A/B comparison, an accessibility audit with implementation-ready recommendations, and a responsive prototype close enough to production that it served as both a testing tool and a stakeholder alignment artifact. The real contribution is reframing what a financing simulator can be: not a standalone calculator, but a modular product surface that supports multiple moments in the vehicle-buying journey."

## Cross-Portfolio Observations

### What works across the portfolio
1. **Genuine complexity.** These are not student projects or side projects. Every case study involves real constraints (legal, technical, organizational, cross-team). This is credible senior-level work.
2. **The risk framework.** Using value/usability/viability/feasibility as a consistent framing device across case studies creates coherence and shows structured thinking.
3. **Honest self-reflection.** The "What I would do differently" sections are consistently strong and credible. They show growth orientation.

### What needs to change across the portfolio
1. **Headlines are all descriptive, never provocative.** Every headline says "I improved X by doing Y." None of them create tension or curiosity. A strong headline should make someone want to read the next paragraph. Lead with the tension, the finding, or the number.
2. **Impact is consistently the weakest section.** Only one case study (Home Charging) has real metrics. For older projects, even directional signals, qualitative shifts, or adoption data would help. "Shipped" is not impact. "Shipped and the team continued using the patterns I created for 18 months" is impact.
3. **Role descriptions consistently undersell.** Phrases like "helped turn," "contributed to," "supported" make the designer sound like a contributor rather than an owner. Where the designer owned the work, the writing should say so directly.
4. **Too much process, not enough decision narrative.** Multiple case studies include detailed process toggles (workshop details, archived artifacts) that add length without adding insight. Replace process documentation with decision stories: "We had three options. Here is why I chose this one."
5. **No clear portfolio arc.** The six case studies do not build a narrative about the designer's growth or specialization. A brief portfolio intro explaining the thread connecting these projects (complex product environments, working with constraints, building foundations for scale) would help a reviewer understand the designer's positioning.

### Verdict as a hiring Head of Design

**Would I advance to the next round?** Conditionally yes, but only if the Home Charging and Financing Simulator case studies are the lead pieces. Those two show the level of product thinking, strategic framing, and domain complexity I look for at a senior level.

The Citadel case studies show solid foundational skills but read as earlier-career work. The FleetUI and MyPowerbox studies are in between: strong raw material that needs sharper framing and harder proof points.

**Priority actions:**
1. Rewrite all six headlines to lead with tension or a number, not a description
2. Strengthen every impact section with any available metric, even directional ones
3. Cut or compress process-detail toggles and replace with decision rationale
4. Move Home Charging Quotation Tool to the first position in the portfolio
5. Add a brief portfolio introduction that frames the designer's specialization
